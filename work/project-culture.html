Whose culture expands in the periphery?

A research project for my Master's at the Faculty of Psychology and Education Sciences, University of Porto (FPCEUP), studying "Cultura em Expansão" — a public cultural policy programme run by Porto City Council in Campanhã, one of the city's peripheral neighbourhoods. The timing mattered: the programme had just gone through a major reformulation, shifting toward an open-call model — so this study captured perceptions right at the moment of that change, comparing what the programme used to be against what it was becoming.

Peripheries often show a more expressive, more inventive path to creativity than an already-established cultural centre ever does.

That belief is what sent me looking for a real case to test it against. Cultura em Expansão runs several of its actions in peripheries from a very central position in the city, which made Campanhã the right place to ask a harder question: does publicly-promoted creativity actually make a periphery feel creative and expressive on its own terms — or does it just arrive there?

// METHODOLOGY — DOUBLE DIAMOND

A design-research process, not a straight line

I structured the investigation using the Double Diamond (Design Council, UK) — borrowed from my design background to give a qualitative study a repeatable shape: opening up to gather material, then narrowing down to make sense of it, twice over.

01 / DISCOVER

Literature review

Grounding the project in theory on cultural policy, periphery and participatory practice.

02 / DEFINE

Mapping + sense-making clusters

Mapped every activity run by the programme, then built meaning clusters and selected who to interview.

03 / DEVELOP

Interviews + photo-elicitation

Semi-structured interviews with 4 stakeholders, plus a photo-elicitation exercise using 18 selected images.

04 / DELIVER

Findings + feedback

Synthesised results and returned them to the community as a public awareness piece.

// MAPPING RESULTS

94 events, mapped and categorised

Before interviewing anyone, I mapped every public activity the programme ran between 2019 and 2024 — the base layer everything else was built on. 10 of the 94 were run by Visões Úteis, the collective most embedded in the territory. Events split into Performance (55), Interaction (29) and Exhibition (10) by what they actually were; "Politics" wasn't a category anything was filed under during mapping — I added it afterwards, deliberately, because the interviews made clear that "political" was itself a lens people used to see the programme, even when no event was explicitly political.

// SAMPLE & ETHICS

Small, deliberately deep, not broad

// FINDINGS

What the research pointed to

01
Trust beats production value.
The spaces that worked were the ones where artists and residents recognised each other over time — not the ones with the biggest budget.
02
The shift to open calls felt like withdrawal.
The programme's recent reformulation toward an open-call model created a sense of disconnection — read by some as institutional retreat from the territory, a kind of "deterritorialisation."
03
Distribution ≠ recognition.
In its current form, the programme functions more as a cultural distributor than as a driver of local recognition.
04
Attendance is a weak signal.
The more meaningful measure of success is whether a programme builds lasting relationships, not how many people showed up.
05
Culture as mediation, not delivery.
Understood as relationship-building rather than a service handed down, cultural practice becomes a form of social and political learning.
// THE DELIVERABLE

An awareness banner, returned to the community

The clearest finding — that partner organisations' creative decisions were seen as apolitical when they in fact carried real political weight — became a public banner: a direct message helping the community itself recognise that taking part in these activities was already a form of political engagement, of cultural politics, whether it was framed that way or not.

AWARENESS BANNER — "THIS ISN'T CONSIDERED POLITICS. BUT IT IS CULTURAL POLITICS."

A QR code on the banner links back to the research findings — taking the same visual language used to promote the programme and turning it into a question about how it's actually perceived.

// WHY THIS MATTERS FOR DESIGN WORK

This project sharpened the research skills I bring into design practice — not just visual output, but the process behind it: mapping a landscape before designing for it, running mixed qualitative methods, coding and clustering open-ended material into usable categories, and designing for trust between groups who don't naturally speak the same language. It's the same muscle I use when a brand or product needs to hold together across very different audiences.
